One story, side by side, across the newsrooms that covered it. Every claim traced back to the sentence it came from.

Nobody has time to read every article about the same story. NewsLens reads them, breaks each one into the factual claims it makes, and shows you which outlets carried which claim. Every claim opens to the exact sentence behind it, in the original article.

There are no bias labels here. No accuracy scores. No verdict about who was right. The site counts what a set of newsrooms did with the same facts, and you decide what that means.

The panel is picked per story, not fixed across the site. A jobs report draws business and economics desks. A sports story draws sports desks, and comparing the two panels would be meaningless. Every story says who is in its panel and why.

Who this is built for NewsLens is built for the reader who already suspects the press is spinning them. That reader will not accept another rating, so the tool does not offer one. It shows the receipts and gets out of the way. If it works for the most suspicious reader in the room, it works for everyone else too.

The part worth arguing with

Every credibility tool has one judgment it cannot avoid making. This is ours, stated plainly rather than buried.

Whoever picks the panel decides what agreement looks like

"Reported by 6 of 6" sounds like arithmetic. It is arithmetic, but only over a list somebody chose. Six outlets make unanimity easy. Thirty would make it rare. Include only business desks and a jobs story looks settled. Add a labour reporter and it may not.

NewsLens refuses to rate outlets, refuses to score accuracy, and refuses to let an AI write explanations. Choosing who gets counted is the one piece of editorial power it still holds, so it is disclosed on every story page rather than treated as a footnote.

What would fix it properly: letting you add or remove an outlet yourself and watch every count move. That is not built yet, and it is the most important thing missing from this prototype.

How a story gets here

Four steps. One model, one human reviewer, and a rule that the reader can always check the work.

1. An AI reads every article

A language model reads every article in that story's panel and lists the factual claims each one makes. It does not judge whether a claim is true.

2. Matching claims are grouped

Claims stating the same fact are grouped together. The corroboration count is nothing more than how many outlets landed in that group. It is arithmetic you can redo by hand.

3. A human checks every extraction

A reviewer checks each claim against the source article using a published checklist. The reviewer only fixes fidelity errors, and never adds, removes, or rewords a claim by preference.

4. Sentences are dated and kept

Outlets edit stories after publishing. Every sentence carries the date it was captured, so a claim can still be checked even after the original article changes.

What NewsLens never does

  • No bias labels. Third-party outlet ratings stay off by default and are never switched on by a reading mode. A political label sitting next to an omission count reads as a verdict, whoever issued it.
  • No truth verdicts. The site shows who reported what. It does not tell you who was right.
  • No generated explanations. Rather than have the AI write a story about why two sentences were grouped, the site shows you both sentences.
  • No accounts, no personalisation, no engagement optimisation. Nothing here learns what you like and gives you more of it.

What is not built

This is a research prototype, and the gaps are part of what it is showing.

Known gaps in this build, disclosed rather than hidden:
  • Five of the six stories are not built. Only the jobs report has an extracted, reviewed claim set.
  • The Methods page, model card, and independence statement are referenced from inside the story page but were never written. A self-audit against published human-AI design guidelines found this to be the single most damaging gap, because it is the one disclosure a reader most needs to reach.
  • Reader flags are recorded in the page but are not transmitted anywhere and do not survive a refresh. There is no reference number and no public log of resolved flags.
  • Corrections made during the first design-phase review pass were not logged as they happened. That gap is disclosed rather than backfilled with a reconstructed number.
  • No wire-service overlap audit has been run, so outlets sharing syndicated copy could still look like independent confirmations.
  • Readers cannot change the outlet panel. Since panel composition decides every count on the page, handing that control to the reader is the most significant unbuilt feature here.
  • No user study has been run. Nothing on this site has been tested with a reader who is not its designer.

No bias labels. No truth verdicts. You judge.

NewsLens is a student research prototype built for HCAI 4304 at Texas Tech University. The claim extraction runs on a commercial language model built by a third party and trained on web-scale data that itself contains media bias. The prototype was designed and built with AI assistance, human-directed, which is disclosed here because the tool holds itself to the same standard it applies to the newsrooms it compares.
